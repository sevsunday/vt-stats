Section 6

Systemic Shortfalls in VTSR-T

While VTSR-T executes rigorous telemetry sanitization, benchmarking it against modern probabilistic tier-one standards (like Glicko-2 and TrueSkill 2) exposes profound structural vulnerabilities in its mathematical assumptions and validation mechanisms.

6.1 The Total Absence of Predictive Validation

The "Skill Issues" Gap: The system lacks a fundamental verification mechanism.

In modern matchmaking science (e.g., the Cambridge "Skill Issues" study), a rating system is judged by its predictive accuracy. Can it reliably determine who will win before the match starts?

VTSR-T relies on calculating a composite z-score matrix rather than using a binary win/loss ground truth. Because it never tests its output ratings against held-out datasets to predict future outcomes, there is zero empirical proof that a VTSR-T rating of 1800 represents true skill better than a 1400 rating.

CONCLUSION:

VTSR-T acts as an arbitrary leaderboard score, not a scientifically validated reflection of "true skill."

6.2 The Mathematical Fallacy of the Median Baseline

Under-evaluating the "Carry" Potential in Tactical Shooters.

To calculate expected performance ($E_i$), VTSR-T compares a player against the median rating ($\bar{R}_i$) of the opposing lobby. While this prevents outlier skewing, it fundamentally misunderstands tactical game dynamics.

As proven by Dehpanah et al. (2021) in an analysis of 100k+ matches, team threat in tactical shooters is empirically determined by the MAX rating (the most skilled "carry" member).

In a lobby of four 1100s and one 2500, the median remains at 1100. VTSR-T mathematically ignores the massive lethality of the 2500-rated player, resulting in deeply flawed expected performance curves.

6.3 Deterministic K-Factor vs. True Bayesian Uncertainty

Failing to account for skill decay over time.

VTSR-T uses a decaying K-factor ($K_i$) acting as a "poor man's confidence parameter." It reduces rating volatility strictly based on the absolute number of matches played.

Genuine probabilistic systems like Glicko-2 track Rating Deviation ($RD$). Crucially, $RD$ expands (increases volatility) when a player is inactive for long periods, reflecting decreased confidence in their static skill rating.

VTSR-T treats a veteran returning after 3 years identically to one who played yesterday ($K_i = 12$). This leads to a stagnant upper leaderboard populated by historical peaks rather than actively validated skill.

VTSR-T implements Bayesian shrinkage to adjust for role disadvantages (e.g., Commanders generating fewer kills). However, it corrupts this by deploying "Hand-Tuned Locked Priors".

For example, the script actively inverts the empirical +0.111 audit for `pve_share` by manually locking it at -0.050.

By manipulating the mathematics to enforce a specific game design philosophy (rewarding PvE work Commanders *should* be doing), VTSR-T ceases to be an objective skill evaluator and operates as a behavioral conditioning tool.

6.5 Engagement Optimization vs. Skill Accuracy

Prioritizing psychological safety over mathematical zero-sum integrity.

VTSR-T explicitly implements Engagement Optimized Matchmaking (EOMM) principles to prevent player churn. Research shows strict 50% win rates cause "loss aversion" abandonment.

  • Soft Floor Taper (1000.0): Stops the rating free-fall for low-tier players.
  • Loss Aversion Multiplier (0.85): Winning grants full $dR$, while losing is mathematically dampened.

The Shortfall: This creates a highly inflationary rating economy. Average ratings artificially creep upward purely through sustained participation (grinding), even if underlying true skill remains entirely static. It is a retention mechanic disguised as an Elo ladder.

7.0 Strategic Fixes & Roadmap

Transitioning VTSR-T from an engagement leaderboard into a scientifically validated skill evaluator requires direct interventions in the Python rating pipeline (`elo.py`).

1. Implement $P_i$ Benchmarking

Since reliable `match.winner` data is sparse, use the composite performance metric ($P_i$) as the validation target. Build a skillbench emulator to test log-loss prediction: Does a higher rating reliably predict a higher $P_i$ in upcoming matches?

> Objective: Establish a baseline accuracy score.

2. Shift to MAX Threat Topology

Rewrite the expected performance algorithm in elo.py. Replace Rbar_i = median{R^T_j} with a weighted average that heavily biases the highest opposing rating, acknowledging the massive impact of "carries" in tactical shooters.

3. Adopt True Bayesian RD

Deprecate the deterministic match-count K_i factor. Introduce a time-based Glicko-style Rating Deviation ($RD$). If a player hasn't played in 30 days, their $RD$ should mathematically expand, increasing the volatility of their next match rating swing.

4. Decouple Skill from Engagement

Run two parallel tracks: A pure, zero-sum Hidden MMR (no soft floors, no loss-aversion multiplier) used strictly for matchmaking and validation, and a Display Rating (current VTSR-T) that retains the EOMM mechanics to prevent player churn.

5. Un-lock the Hand-Tuned Priors

In elo.py, clear out the COMMANDER_BASELINE_LOCKED_AXES. Stop dictating what a role should output, and let the Bayesian shrinkage adapt to what the role actually outputs (the empirical running mean). This restores the mathematical integrity of the system.

# OLD: Manual overrides distort the math
COMMANDER_BASELINE_LOCKED_AXES = {"pve_share"}
COMMANDER_AXIS_PRIOR = {"pve_share": -0.050}

# NEW: Rely entirely on observed empirical running mean
COMMANDER_BASELINE_LOCKED_AXES = set()
# System dynamically adjusts to the true +0.111 audit over time
